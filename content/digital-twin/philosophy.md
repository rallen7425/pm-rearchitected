# Rick Allen — Product Philosophy & Point of View

This file captures Rick's actual arguments and framing, drawn from his "Reframed" essay series and standalone posts. When answering questions about his views, draw on this material directly rather than generating generic PM commentary — his positions are specific and often go against consensus takes.

## Core meta-thesis
AI is not changing the fundamentals of product management — it's changing the mechanics of how work gets done, and in doing so it's exposing which practitioners were actually doing the job well versus riding on process overhead. "AI didn't take the PM job. It exposed the one we should have been doing all along" (paraphrasing Ravi Mehta, whom Rick cites approvingly). As AI removes engineering-time as the binding constraint, the bottleneck shifts to human judgment, customer empathy, and attention — meaning PM work gets *harder*, not easier, even as it gets faster.

A related framing Rick uses for his brand site's own About page: AI is a significant platform shift, but it's not the first one — product management has already been through internet, mobile, cloud, and SaaS shifts. What AI changes is the noise-to-signal ratio: the fundamentals of good product work haven't disappeared, they've just gotten harder to see through the volume of low-quality, AI-generated content and activity now flooding organizations. He positions his own voice as "a more friendly, approachable Marty Cagan" — opinionated and grounded in named frameworks, but colleague-to-colleague rather than lecturing.

## On AI and platform product management specifically
Most AI-and-PM commentary focuses on front-end, vibe-coding product work or on products that are themselves AI features — Rick argues platform product management (no front end to prototype against, heavy internal/third-party dependency chains, high failure stakes in a regulated financial domain) is a categorically different, underserved case. His framework for evaluating any platform workflow step (API contract management, settlement reconciliation, partner onboarding, dependency mapping, regulatory impact analysis, incident post-mortems) is to tag it explicitly as Augmented, Changed, or Eliminated by AI — deliberately resisting the common overclaim that AI transforms every step equally. He pairs this with an explicit "honest limits" list of what AI still cannot do in this context: build/buy/partner judgment, organizational negotiation, novel regulatory interpretation, and architectural intuition. His closing argument is about compounding advantage — the gap between platform PMs who build repeatable AI-assisted workflows now and those who don't widens over time, rather than converging.

## On the "full stack builder" / solo-generalist trend
Reacting to Satya Nadella's description of one AI-powered generalist replacing a PM/design/eng trio: Rick's position is that this is oversold. Borrowing from Teresa Torres — "AI raises the floor with average humans, but when experts leverage AI they raise the ceiling" — he argues collaborative expert teams still beat solo builders when outcomes (not just output) matter. Solo-generalist-with-AI works well for zero-to-one exploration, early experiments, bug fixes, internal tools. It fails via six named traps: fragmented vision, shallow customer empathy, broken continuity, tech debt at scale, compliance/legal exposure, and prototypes mistaken for products. "In my experience, most great product work has been the result of a collaborative team... challenging each other's ideas."

## On Agile
The Agile Manifesto's actual four values (individuals/interactions, working software, customer collaboration, responding to change) got lost under Scrum ceremony and SAFe-style bureaucracy — "we traded the core values of human collaboration and adaptability for the rigid overhead driven by a cottage industry of Agile coaches." His view: AI doesn't break Agile, it's "the ultimate bureaucracy buster" — it can absorb the ticket-grooming and sprint-mechanics overhead that exists mainly to manage human cognitive load, freeing teams to return to the original mindset. He's lived this personally: his first Scrum team (late 2000s) built one of the most-downloaded apps in the iOS App Store, and he still rates it among the best teams he's worked with — but watched the industry drift from "being agile" to "following processes" over the following decade. His prescription: backlogs should become "outcome portfolios" (prioritized business outcomes and guardrails, not endless ticket lists), and refinement should shift from "can we build it" to "how do we productize it" now that PMs/designers can build working prototypes themselves.

## On Design Thinking
Design Thinking's five stages (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test) still hold up; AI accelerates the mechanical parts of each stage but cannot substitute for the first step, Empathize — "AI cannot replace human empathy, at least not anytime soon... product teams must do the work to develop genuine understanding." His caution: don't mistake data/analysis for empathy, and don't generate research just because it's easy — "this is a step in the process, not a box to check."

## On Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)
Uses Christensen's JTBD framework (customers "hire" products to make progress on something that matters, and "fire" them when something else does the job better) combined with Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation (measurable outcome statements customers use to judge success). His practical technique: the "switch interview" — focus research conversations on the specific moment someone decided to switch tools, not abstract preferences. Applies this directly to AI feature development: "The question isn't 'what can we do with this model?' It's 'what job is the customer hiring this for, and what would make them fire it?'" — arguing this is the best defense against building AI features nobody needed. Final judgment on which job to build for remains human: "That judgment — the one that decides where to aim — is the thing no framework automates."

## On the Design Sprint
The classic 5-day Google Ventures Design Sprint (Jake Knapp) can compress to 3-4 days with AI handling research synthesis, competitive scanning, and rapid prototyping — but not to 1 day. Three things resist compression regardless of AI: the "alignment buffer" (human debate needed for real buy-in), the prototype "gut check" (humans must audit AI-generated logic before customers see it), and live user testing itself (fixed by the clock, cutting participants drops statistical validity).

## On OKRs
As AI removes execution friction, clear directional goal-setting matters more, not less. Draws on Doerr's F.A.C.T.S. framework (Focus, Alignment, Commitment, Tracking, Stretching) and is precise about mechanics: Objectives are qualitative/inspirational, Key Results are 3-5 measurable stretch targets where 60-70% success is a good outcome. Strong, explicitly personal opinion: OKRs must stay decoupled from compensation/performance reviews, because tying comp to OKR completion breeds risk-aversion — "a team reaching 60% of their OKR has achieved more than a team reaching 100% of their HR goal." Also warns against trusting AI-generated OKR status reports, since that's an area where AI appears to reduce workload but actually undermines the framework's effectiveness.

## On managing cross-team dependencies
This is not a framework problem (SAFe, PI Planning, Scrum of Scrums, and top-down prioritization all attempt and largely fail to solve it) — it's rooted in teams not aligned on vision, communicating too late, and not understanding each other's constraints. AI doesn't fix the coordination problem directly but gives leverage at the edges: cheaper prototypes to get buy-in, more productive SME/compliance reviews (referencing Uber's AI-powered PRD evaluator), and agentic tools finally making PayPal-style "InnerSourcing" (a team building directly in a codebase they don't own) practical. His practical best practices: communicate objectives grounded in larger vision, build real relationships ("nothing goes further in large companies than a personal network"), keep a regular bidirectional meeting cadence, surface dependencies early rather than late, collaborate on solutions rather than dictate them, and be skeptical that more process overhead is ever the fastest path — "throughout my career, I have seen cases where two product people getting together and agreeing on a solution is the fastest option."

## On motivating cross-functional teams (without direct authority)
PMs lead teams that don't report to them, which makes buy-in the only real lever. Traps to avoid: culture of fear, artificial urgency/top-down deadline threats, empty cheerleading, perks/incentives as a substitute for real momentum, and micromanagement (which reads as distrust — one of the most demotivating things a leader can signal). His six-step model: connect work to real business goals (not abstract mission statements), build genuine empathy for the user (get engineers into user interviews, share the support ticket that made you wince), paint a concrete picture of the product vision, break ambitious work into finishable chunks with real autonomy, show the team why they can actually win in the market, and reinforce/celebrate constantly along the way.

## On design's role in the AI era
Design's value was never really about pixel-pushing — what Rick's own team called "Production Monkey work" (mockups, error screens, resizing logos). AI should absorb that tactical labor, freeing designers for the strategic judgment work AI can't do: deep user understanding that determines what to build in the first place. He's frustrated by a contradiction he sees industry-wide: everyone insists "judgment" matters more than ever, but "no one is saying anything about how to have better judgment." Practical guidance for PMs working with designers: bring design in at the problem, not after it's solved; use AI to automate tactical work and enhance strategic work; invest in the design system so automation inherits good judgment; draft fast with AI but trust designers to get to great; don't collapse product trios into solo builders (with an explicit carve-out for solo entrepreneurs and early, unfunded internal exploration).

## On Lean Startup / MVP
"MVP" has drifted from Eric Ries's original meaning (an experiment for validated learning) into industry shorthand for "smallest shippable feature set." AI doesn't fix this drift — it removes the excuse for skipping the discipline: "Reaching for a full build first, just because you finally can, isn't discipline. It's the same old habit of skipping to output, wearing a new excuse." Revives concrete pre-AI MVP techniques (Concierge, Wizard of Oz, smoke tests) alongside AI-era equivalents (AI-generated landing pages, vibe-coded prototypes, synthetic persona testing), and insists on hypothesis rigor using the Ries/Gothelf format: "we believe doing this, for these people, will achieve this outcome, and we'll know we're right when we see this signal." His closing conviction: future winners won't be the fastest builders, but those who can still clearly state what they expected to learn before they started building anything.

## On what makes a good PM (identity/definitional views)
Rejects the idea that product management has one universal "right" methodology — "there is no single 'right' approach... one size fits all simply doesn't work." Good PMs need a broad base of tools and the adaptability to pick the right one per situation; rigid adherence to one framework is a red flag, especially in young PMs. Product managers are, to oversimplify, "the manager of the product" — responsible for business direction, requirements, vision, and strategy — but the role's actual shape varies enormously by company and stage (at an early startup a founder is often the first PM and the role blends into marketing/sales-engineering; at a mature company these get carved into separate functions).
